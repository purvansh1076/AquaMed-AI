```markdown
### 2.1 AI-Based Agricultural Water Management
The application of artificial intelligence in agricultural water management has evolved from rule-based expert systems to data-driven predictive architectures [1]. Modern computational irrigation models predominantly leverage sensor telemetry to optimize water application depths. However, existing literature focuses primarily on on-farm precision irrigation (e.g., automated drip valves) rather than communal canal dispute mediation [2].

### 2.2 Water Demand Prediction
Accurate estimation of crop water demand forms the cornerstone of sound allocation. The standard benchmark established by the Food and Agriculture Organization is the FAO-56 Penman-Monteith method [3]. Recent studies have demonstrated that gradient boosted trees (XGBoost) [6] and ensemble random forests [5] can approximate non-linear agricultural demand with high fidelity when trained on meteorological and phenological inputs [4].

### 2.3 Optimization-Based Water Allocation
Operations research has extensively addressed water resources allocation using Linear Programming (LP), Non-Linear Programming (NLP), and Genetic Algorithms [7]. Google OR-Tools GLOP solver provides efficient simplex implementations for continuous linear programs [8]. A known limitation in classical allocation optimization is the treatment of human farmers as passive, compliant entities rather than self-interested decision-makers [9].

### 2.4 Multi-Agent Systems in Common-Pool Resources
Elinor Ostrom’s pioneering principles on governing common-pool resources emphasize that decentralized, rule-governed user monitoring frequently outperforms top-down administrative quotas [10]. Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) provide a computational paradigm to simulate autonomous, heterogenous actors competing for finite commons [11].

### 2.5 Automated Negotiation and Conflict Resolution
Automated negotiation protocols establish mathematical foundations for agent bargaining. Rooted in cooperative game theory, Nash Bargaining establishes axioms for equitable surplus distribution [12]. Practical automated mediation relies on monotonic concession protocols and utility reservation thresholds, commonly termed the Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement [13].

### 2.6 Explainable AI in High-Stakes Domains
Complex machine learning models often operate as opaque predictors. The SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations) framework, based on cooperative game theory, provides local feature importance attributions by calculating marginal contributions across all feature coalitions [14]. In agricultural and legal domains, explainability is essential to establish trust among stakeholders [15].
```
